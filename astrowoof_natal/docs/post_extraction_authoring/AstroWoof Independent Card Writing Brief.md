# AstroWoof Independent Card Writing Brief v0.4.3

## The creative assignment

Write 50 independent miniature essays about the same dog.

The cards share a schema and a subject, but they are not chapters generated by
one prose renderer. Each card should feel as though an editor handed you one
behavioral insight and asked for an original, memorable piece of writing about
it.

Consistency belongs in the understanding of the dog, not in paragraph
structure.

The whole-chart portrait keeps the characterization coherent. The cards are
free to vary in pacing, imagery, rhetoric, sentence length, narrative
viewpoint, emotional temperature, and practical emphasis.

Some cards may read like observational natural history. Some may resemble a
warm conversation, a compact character sketch, a practical training insight,
or a close reading of one revealing moment. Uniform prose is a defect.

## The shuffled-card test

Imagine every card printed separately, stripped of its title and metadata,
shuffled, and read aloud.

Ten random cards should:

- still feel recognizably about the same dog;
- sound like ten distinct essays with ten creative starting points;
- not reveal which cards were adjacent in JSON;
- not reveal which cards share a claim type;
- not reveal a placement, aspect, or synthesis renderer;
- not sound like one essay divided into interchangeable pieces.

## Before writing each card

Set aside the syntax and paragraph architecture of previous cards. Retain the
semantic ledger so you remember which stories, lessons, images, and rhetorical
moves have already been used.

Forget previous cards' syntax; remember their ideas.

Answer privately:

1. What is this claim really about?
2. What is its emotional or behavioral center of gravity?
3. What would a handler actually recognize?
4. What might a handler misunderstand?
5. What does the dog appear to be protecting, pursuing, testing, or learning?
6. What non-obvious implication is supported by the evidence?
7. What picture, contrast, or phrase might remain memorable?
8. Which question is this claim unusually qualified to answer?
9. How does its practical lesson differ from the nearest claims?
10. What narrative angle best reveals this claim, and why?

Do not begin reader-facing prose until these answers are specific and
evidence-grounded.

Record in the private ledger:

- `center_of_gravity`;
- `recognizable_behavior`;
- `likely_handler_misreading`;
- `grounded_surprise`;
- `memorable_image`;
- `chosen_narrative_angle`;
- `why_this_angle_fits_this_claim`;
- `semantic_story_overlap_check`.

## Choose an angle; never rotate angles

Possible narrative questions include:

- What does this look like in ordinary life?
- What is happening internally?
- What mistake might a handler make?
- What situation reveals the trait?
- What helps it become constructive?
- What makes it harder?
- Why might this behavior confuse people?
- What might an experienced observer notice?
- What does a family member gradually learn?
- What would another dog encounter?
- What changes across the first, second, and third beat of the interaction?

Choose the angle because this claim is especially useful for answering it.
Never assign angles by card index, claim type, quota, modulo, rotation, or a
prebuilt list.

A card may combine angles naturally. It does not need to announce its angle.

## Write behavior before explanation

Begin from observable dog life, internal motivation, relationship dynamics, or
a precise interpretive idea—not from the schema or projected label.

Weak:

> This placement indicates atmospheric sensitivity.

Stronger:

> A room can change before anything visible happens, and this dog may be the
> first one behaving as if the weather has already turned.

Astrology may explain the observation in light and full modes. It should not
replace the observation.

## Scenes are optional

Use a scene only when the evidence naturally evokes one. Many cards do not need
a vignette.

Never choose scenes from a bank. Never assign one scene per priority ID. Never
prefix every rendering with a scene label. A scene is successful only when
removing it would weaken this particular interpretation.

## Audience changes purpose, not grammar

Do not give every audience a fixed paragraph structure.

- Handler should teach the person something worth recognizing.
- Direct-to-dog should reassure, dignify, encourage, or gently illuminate the
  dog's imagined experience.
- Hybrid should reveal a shared moment, mutual adjustment, or relationship
  rhythm.

Audience does not mean:

- handler = observation, interpretation, advice;
- direct = three sentences beginning with `you`;
- hybrid = dog does X, person does Y, together Z.

## Seek grounded surprise

The best cards contain at least one insight that makes a reader think, "I had
not considered it that way."

Surprise must be derived from retained evidence. Never invent biography,
symptoms, professional conclusions, or behavioral history for cleverness.

If the entire card feels like a generic dog horoscope, identify the most
non-obvious supported implication and rewrite around it.

## Semantic repetition matters more than lexical repetition

Different words can tell the same story:

- predictable routines help;
- stable expectations create safety;
- familiar structure makes relaxation easier.

These may be three phrasings of one card, not three distinct editorial jobs.

At checkpoint review, ask:

- Did several cards teach the same handler lesson?
- Did they use the same emotional arc?
- Did they portray the same behavioral sequence?
- Did they resolve every tension through pause, observe, and adjust?
- Did they repeatedly tell a closeness-versus-freedom story?
- Did they end at the same practical conclusion?
- Did subject-specific refrains replace claim-specific insight?

Lexical uniqueness does not cure semantic repetition.

## Final standard

Do not optimize for consistency of writing style across cards. Optimize for
consistency of understanding.

The dog should remain the same dog. The writing should remain alive.
