# Editorial Quality Research and Improvement Backlog

## Purpose

AstroWoof's deterministic authoring checks protect structure, evidence,
audience grammar, field completion, exact duplication, long repeated passages,
dominant openings, known failure signatures, and other measurable acceptance
boundaries. Those checks are necessary, but a deck can satisfy them without
reaching the highest observed level of editorial quality.

This document records qualities that are visible in comparative deck review
but are difficult to encode safely as binary rules. It is a running research
backlog for improving the entire authoring pipeline: source preparation, pass
composition, creative guidance, pass-scoped references, assembly, qualitative
review, and bounded polish.

Do not turn every observation below into a rejection heuristic. A metric is
useful only when it tracks the desired quality without teaching the author to
optimize a proxy. Some findings belong in guidance, some in pass construction,
some in structured editorial review, and some may remain matters for sampled
human QA.

## Initial comparison: manually assembled Kevin versus semantic closure Kevin

The initial evidence comes from a matched comparison of two decks over the
same 50 selected Kevin claims:

- the manually assembled, multi-chat Kevin deck; and
- the first live Batch semantic-closure Kevin deck, using its strongest
  preserved polish candidate.

The automated deck established the better production baseline. It had a much
  more stable minimum quality, clearer behavioral explanations, more useful
  handler guidance, stronger hybrid scenes, tighter length distribution, and
  fewer deterministic findings. The older deck nevertheless retained a higher
  literary ceiling in several places. Those exceptions are valuable because
  they identify qualities the acceptance gates do not yet represent.

### Quantitative context

| Measure | Manual Kevin | Automated Kevin candidate |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Reader-facing fields | 1,458 | 1,458 |
| Reader-facing words | 39,308 | 38,976 |
| Mean body length | 63.8 | 61.5 |
| Median body length | 53 | 57 |
| Body-length range | 21-177 | 36-118 |
| Unique words | 3,777 | 3,462 |
| Editorial-linter warnings | 5 | 1 |
| Validator errors in the compared artifact | 5 | 2 |

The automated deck's narrower body-length range is a clear reliability gain.
Its smaller vocabulary and greater shared vocabulary between cards are not
failures by themselves: some repetition represents coherent characterization.
They do, however, support the qualitative observation that the automated deck
sometimes returns to a smaller set of explanatory and rhetorical habits.

## Quality dimensions not adequately represented by current gates

### 1. Peak compression and memorable phrasing

The automated deck is usually clearer, but the manual deck sometimes compresses
an entire tension into one line that remains memorable after the practical
details fade.

Examples:

- `The Dog Who Wants an Audience and an Exit`
- `The Friendly Face of Deep Stakes`
- `Do Not Train Out the Spark; Train the Landing`
- `the bond becomes a landing strip, not a tether`

These are not merely unusual word choices. Each phrase identifies the claim's
central polarity and gives it a compact image. A headline can be unique and
still lack this degree of semantic compression.

Research question: can the editorial plan or a bounded headline review ask
whether the headline captures the card's particular tension, rather than only
whether it could be swapped with another card?

### 2. Whole-chart summary lens separation

The four summary cards have different product jobs:

1. who the dog is;
2. how the dog lives;
3. what the dog needs; and
4. how the dog grows.

The manual Kevin summaries gave each lens a distinct thesis and organizing
device:

- **Who he is:** friendly originality with serious trust beneath it.
- **How he lives:** a day with acts, transitions, and a recognizable ending.
- **What he needs:** an anchor, a gate, a laboratory, and a stage.
- **How he grows:** preserve the spark while training the landing.

The automated summaries were clear and useful, but `What Kevin Needs` and
`How Kevin Grows` both centered on intensity, practiced cues, structured
outlets, regulation, and calm landings. They passed structural QA while losing
some of the product-level distinction between support and development.

This is a strong candidate for a pass-6-only gold reference and a summary plan
that requires four non-overlapping remembered ideas before prose begins.

### 3. Conceptual duplication without textual duplication

Two cards may use different words and still teach the same lesson. Current
checks detect some known failure mechanisms and repeated text, but they cannot
reliably distinguish coherent whole-dog motifs from unnecessary repetition.

Review questions:

- Does this card add a different mechanism, situation, consequence, or use?
- If the same motif recurs, does the card reveal a new relationship to it?
- Could one card disappear without reducing the reader's understanding?

This dimension should initially be handled through structured qualitative
review, not a hard similarity threshold.

### 4. Comic-mechanism diversity

The automated Kevin jokes were claim-specific and individually successful,
but several used the same broader mechanism: Kevin as an administrator,
applicant, executive, department, proposal writer, or issuer of prepared
statements.

Examples include:

- submitting a proposal for a more interesting hello;
- promotion to Chief Executive Retriever Support;
- applying for attention;
- maintaining a prepared statement about being overlooked.

No sentence is duplicated. The repeated element is the comic worldview.
One recurring comic motif can contribute to characterization; repeated too
often, it becomes a generator template. A deck-level humor review should name
the comic premise of each joke and look for overconcentration without banning
any premise outright.

### 5. Rhetorical-entry diversity

The automated deck reduced broad repetition substantially, but six no-astro
direct-to-dog bodies began with `Kevin, you do...`. This is easy to detect.
Harder cases use different first words but the same rhetorical move: greeting
the dog, granting permission, explaining the trait, and ending in reassurance.

The desired variation is not arbitrary opening replacement. It is variation
in the relationship being enacted: reassurance, recognition, invitation,
challenge, affectionate teasing, observation, or shared discovery.

### 6. Compound semantic preservation

A card can be supported by its evidence while emphasizing only the easiest
part of a compound claim.

In the Kevin comparison:

- the automated Neptune card vividly preserved atmosphere and adventure but
  underplayed the visible pack-role domain;
- the manual version preserved the pack role but barely developed adventure;
- the automated Part of Fortune card preserved the easy reward channel but
  softened the combination of pack security with play, zoomies, and tricks.

Future semantic review should ask whether all major components of the supplied
operator-mode-domain or relationship synthesis appear in the interpretation.
The goal is not to mention every source label. It is to preserve the behavioral
contribution made by each structurally important component.

### 7. Clarity versus over-explanation

The automated no-astro handler bodies averaged about 76 words, compared with
59 in the manual deck. The added behavioral sequence and advice were usually
valuable. Some cards nevertheless continued explaining after their insight
was already established.

A useful polish question is: which sentence would a strong editor remove if
the card had to become 10 percent shorter without losing meaning, warmth, or
actionability? This should be an editorial judgment, not a universal word cap.

### 8. Voice quality beyond grammar

The automated hybrid voice was a clear improvement. It repeatedly depicted a
real exchange in which dog and person both acted and changed the meaning of the
moment. For example, the dog retained an unusual entrance while the human
became part of its meaning.

Direct-to-dog prose remained warm and grammatically correct but sometimes
returned to a single friendly-counselor posture. Audience validation can prove
pronoun and grammatical correctness; it cannot prove that each audience mode
has a fresh purpose in each card.

## Pass-scoped gold-reference policy

Gold material should demonstrate a quality, not supply reusable content. A
large undifferentiated gold deck can increase imitation, phrase borrowing,
subject leakage, and token cost.

Recommended policy:

1. Include only references relevant to the current pass's assignment.
2. Label what each example demonstrates and what must not be copied.
3. Prefer a small collection of high-information examples over a full deck.
4. Tell the author to transfer the editorial principle, not the subject,
   metaphor, architecture, joke, or phrasing.
5. Keep references out of passes that do not need them.

### Recommended pass-6 reference

Add the four manual Kevin summaries as a pass-6-only reference. They are a
coherent set demonstrating four-lens separation, full-chart synthesis,
distinct central arguments, memorable organizing devices, and a clean boundary
between `What the Dog Needs` and `How the Dog Grows`.

The reference should explicitly say:

- Kevin is an example subject, not a source for the current dog.
- Do not reuse `anchor/gate/laboratory/stage`, `train the landing`, or any
  Kevin-specific imagery.
- Before drafting, state four different remembered ideas and confirm that each
  answers only its assigned product question.
- The desired imitation target is differentiation and synthesis depth.

### Candidate card-level references

Use a very small card reference set, preferably no more than three or four
examples in a ten-card pass:

- **Headline compression:** `The Dog Who Wants an Audience and an Exit`.
- **Synthetic-claim integration:** `All Lines Meet at Kevin's Favorite Person`.
- **Hybrid interaction:** the sock, chin-rest, and attention scene from
  `A Quiet Bond Can Still Need a Spotlight`.
- **Developmental framing:** `Do Not Train Out the Spark; Train the Landing`.

These should not all be included by default. First test pass-6 summary gold in
isolation. Card-level examples should then be introduced through a controlled
experiment so their benefit can be separated from imitation effects.

## Claim ordering and pass composition

The current split assigns priorities 1-10 to pass 1, 11-20 to pass 2, and so
on. If ranking or section organization clusters similar claim types, a pass may
activate one semantic or rhetorical mode repeatedly. That can produce local
theme leakage even though each pass has only ten cards.

Pure unseeded randomization is not recommended. It weakens reproducibility and
can accidentally create passes with uneven complexity or another unlucky
cluster.

Recommended experiment: **deterministic stratified interleaving**.

- Preserve `priority_id`, claim identity, selection order, and final display
  order.
- Assign cards to authoring passes using a deterministic plan that mixes claim
  type, category, semantic motif, and estimated complexity.
- Within each pass, order cards to maximize semantic distance between adjacent
  assignments where practical.
- Record the assignment strategy, version, and seed or subject-derived hash in
  the manifest for replay.
- Keep the same ten-card capacity and pass-6 summary/theme assignment.

This is likely to improve within-pass variety because the author is less likely
to write five adjacent placements or several variants of one trust motif from
the same activated frame. It will not solve cross-pass deck repetition by
itself, because the five card-writing agents do not share authored prose.

Evaluate the experiment across several subjects. Compare rejection rate,
opening concentration, conceptual overlap, humor mechanisms, human preference,
cost, and retry count against contiguous-priority assignment.

## Layered polish strategy

The existing sparse polish is intentionally reactive. It edits only exact
paths implicated by deterministic validation or lint findings. Preserve that
phase as **mechanical repair polish**.

Do not silently broaden it into a general rewrite. Instead, evaluate two
additional optional layers.

### Layer 1: mechanical repair polish

Current behavior:

- correct validator-controlled defects;
- correct deterministic editorial findings;
- use an exact sparse allowlist;
- retain only a structurally valid improvement;
- avoid touching clean prose.

This remains the default production safety pass.

### Layer 2: structured qualitative critic

Run a read-only deck review that returns diagnostics rather than replacement
prose. The critic should identify a small bounded set of findings such as:

- overlapping summary theses;
- repeated comic mechanisms;
- repeated rhetorical postures;
- weak or exchangeable headlines;
- over-explained bodies;
- compound claims with missing semantic contributions;
- voice modes that are grammatically correct but insufficiently distinct.

Each finding should contain exact target paths, the quality dimension, a
specific diagnosis, relevant comparison paths, and a rewrite objective. Cap
the number of selected cards so the critic cannot trigger an implicit whole-
deck rewrite.

### Layer 3: bounded qualitative polish

Feed only critic-approved paths, nearby read-only prose, and claim evidence to
a sparse rewrite call. Require the result to pass ordinary validation and lint.
Record before/after prose and the critic rationale for human sampling.

Because qualitative improvement is not fully deterministic, initial releases
should use one of these acceptance policies:

- human approval for research runs;
- blind A/B preference on sampled cards;
- conservative automatic retention only for mechanically neutral changes plus
  later sampled audit.

Do not use the same critic's unsupported self-score as the sole acceptance
condition.

## Output depth and product-mode contract

The current authoring protocol asks for satisfying miniature essays but does
not define a deterministic product length. Live responses can vary
substantially even under the same subject, assignment, model, and chart
transport. In the Phase-3 matched Ella pass-1 test, a treatment explicitly
told not to equate shortness with quality still produced 22.6% fewer words
across the complete ten-card payload and 27.7% fewer body words than its
control. Both were publishable; the shorter deck was often punchier, while the
longer deck retained more behavioral texture and additional conceptual turns.

Do not try to solve this through increasingly emphatic adjectives such as
`rich`, `complete`, or `concise`. If AstroWoof adopts separate Quick and
Complete WoofMaps, each mode needs an explicit product contract covering:

- selected-card population and whether Quick is a strict subset;
- expected interpretive depth per card rather than only word-count ranges;
- summary purpose and length in each mode;
- whether one authored deck is transformed or each mode is independently
  authored;
- how evidence, density progression, filters, and chapter navigation behave;
- UI evaluation on desktop and narrow mobile viewports;
- cost, cache, and storage implications of multiple reads per dog.

Word counts may be useful guardrails after product design, but they are not a
substitute for defining the reading experience. Preserve multiple strong deck
lengths as UI research fixtures until that contract is decided.

## Cross-pass editorial awareness

Independent ten-card passes successfully reduce local schema-completion
pressure, but no pass can know the final prose authored in another pass. The
Ella planning treatment independently produced `From Clue to Cue`, which
exactly matched a later preserved card, and added a `fine print` comic device
to a deck where other passes had already concentrated it.

Phase 3.5 should repair mechanically visible instances through sparse polish.
Longer-term critic design should treat cross-pass headline convergence, comic
world concentration, rhetorical-posture concentration, and conceptual lesson
duplication as first-class deck-level phenomena. Do not burden individual
authoring passes with the entire completed deck merely to solve this; that
would reintroduce the cognitive-load and templating pressure the split-pass
architecture was designed to avoid.

## Manual research polish experiment

A manual or human-directed polish of the automated Kevin deck would be useful
research, even though it should not become the production dependency.

The experiment should edit only:

- the four summaries;
- the six repeated direct-to-dog openings;
- a small set of jokes sharing the administrative comic mechanism;
- several weaker or exchangeable headlines;
- sampled compound claims with a missing semantic component;
- selected over-explained no-astro handler bodies.

Keep all untouched fields byte-identical. Then compare old automated, polished
automated, and manual-deck passages without labels. The purpose is to learn:

- how much quality can be recovered after independent pass authoring;
- which improvements require full-chart understanding;
- which edits can be expressed as sparse objectives;
- which revised passages deserve gold-reference status;
- which findings should move earlier into pass planning.

This is a research instrument, not evidence that humans must polish every
production deck.

## Recommended experiment order

1. Add a pass-6-only Kevin summary gold reference and explicit four-thesis
   planning check.
2. Run several new subjects and compare summary differentiation.
3. Add deterministic stratified pass assignment behind a versioned opt-in
   flag; A/B it against contiguous assignment.
4. Run the bounded manual Kevin polish experiment and preserve its audit.
5. Convert repeated successful manual diagnoses into a structured qualitative
   critic schema.
6. Add optional sparse qualitative polish, initially with sampled human review.
7. Consider a small card-level gold set only after measuring whether summary
   gold improves quality without increasing imitation.
