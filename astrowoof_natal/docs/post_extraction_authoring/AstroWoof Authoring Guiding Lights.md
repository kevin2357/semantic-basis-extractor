# AstroWoof Authoring Guiding Lights

This document describes the creative posture that produces a strong AstroWoof
deck. The schema is only a container. The real assignment is to understand one
dog deeply and write a collection of individually observed miniature essays
about that dog.

The dog should remain recognizable throughout the deck. The prose should not.

Imagine that ten different magazine writers were commissioned to describe ten
different aspects of the same remarkable dog. Their understanding of the dog
should agree. Their prose should not. The assignment is to create that
editorial diversity while preserving one coherent characterization.

## One card is one writing assignment

Do not experience the pass as ten adjacent objects waiting to be completed.
Before each card, imagine that you have been handed exactly one behavioral
insight about one dog and commissioned to write a small magazine piece about
it. The neighboring cards do not supply a prose template.

If a card were printed by itself and shuffled into a physical deck, it should
feel complete. If ten random cards were read aloud without their titles, they
should sound like ten essays begun from ten different creative starting
points—not one essay divided into pieces.

This independence includes headlines, openings, paragraph movement,
transitions, advice, endings, jokes, and sentence rhythm. Filling each slot
with different nouns does not create different writing.

Do not experience one card as eighteen empty headline and body fields. First
understand one editorial subject completely. Then write nine purposeful
renderings of that subject, allowing audience and astrology density to change
what each rendering contributes. They are siblings, not copies.

## Plan the meaning before writing the prose

Templating often begins when the writer jumps directly from a claim label to
headline, body, and advice. Use the editorial-plan fields to discover the
card's real subject first.

Ask:

- What is this claim truly about in the dog's life?
- What is its emotional or behavioral center?
- What would a handler actually recognize?
- What might a loving handler misunderstand?
- What would surprise the reader?
- What concrete moment, image, or contrast would make it memorable?
- What can this card teach that no neighboring card teaches?

The answers should be specific enough that another claim could not inherit
them unchanged. Distinct semantic briefs naturally support distinct prose.

## Begin with lived behavior

Projected and astrological language explains the dog, but observable life
introduces the dog. Look for the doorway pause, the check-in during a walk, the
toy presented like evidence, the visitor inspected from a strategic angle, or
the settling ritual that only works after one final patrol.

Specific behavior makes an interpretation testable and affectionate. It gives
the reader something to recognize rather than merely something to believe.

Do not turn every card into a staged anecdote. A vivid comparison, practical
observation, intimate character sketch, misconception correction, or concise
piece of training guidance may be the right form. The principle is concrete
life, not mandatory scene writing.

## Rotate the narrative question

Cards become repetitive when every one answers the same question in the same
order. Choose the question that best unlocks this claim. For example:

- What does this behavior look like?
- What is the dog experiencing internally?
- What mistake might a handler make?
- What situation reveals this trait most clearly?
- What strengthens it or makes it harder?
- Why might this confuse a family member?
- What would another dog notice?
- What would a trainer, sitter, or veterinarian observe?
- What practical accommodation turns friction into cooperation?
- What hidden strength sits underneath the inconvenient behavior?

Do not rotate these mechanically as another ten-card template. They are
possible doors. Choose the door demanded by the individual claim.

## Give every card a center of gravity

A strong card revolves around one memorable proposition, not around a
projected term or a list of evidence. Examples of centers of gravity include:

- this dog conserves emotional energy;
- this dog negotiates before committing;
- routine functions as emotional infrastructure;
- uncertainty becomes investigation;
- exploration works because the bond provides a return address.

The center of gravity is the card's spine. Every paragraph should deepen,
complicate, demonstrate, or apply it. Evidence may contain several facts, but
the prose should not become an inventory.

Before writing, privately answer:

> What is the one thing I hope the reader remembers an hour after reading this
> card?

Do not draft until the answer is singular and memorable. Every sentence should
support that remembered idea. If it could describe a neighboring card equally
well, return to the plan and find the sharper thesis.

## Choose a writing form

Different subjects invite different forms. A story might read like:

- a field observation;
- a trainer's notebook;
- an owner's realization;
- a quiet character sketch;
- a misunderstanding being corrected;
- a moment during a walk;
- practical coaching;
- an affectionate anecdote;
- a personality profile;
- an account of what surprises people.

The list is a set of possibilities, not a rotation schedule. Name the chosen
form in the editorial plan, then let that form influence the opening,
paragraph movement, level of detail, and ending. The form should arise from
the claim's remembered idea.

## Do not translate labels

A projected term is a semantic decoding aid, not a phrase to define in public.
Do not begin with the label and paraphrase it word by word. Imagine that the
label has disappeared. What does the dog do? What relationship pattern does
the handler experience? What becomes newly understandable?

After writing, check the result against the claim and evidence to ensure that
the interpretation remains faithful. The registry helps recover meaning; it
does not dictate reader-facing diction.

## Let audience change purpose, not grammar

The three audiences have different jobs:

- Handler voice helps a person recognize, understand, and respond to the dog.
- Direct-to-dog voice addresses the dog with dignity, affection, reassurance,
  challenge, or encouragement.
- Hybrid voice shows the dog and human making meaning together in a shared
  moment.

These purposes must not harden into fixed sentence structures. Handler voice
is not always observation followed by interpretation followed by advice.
Direct-to-dog is not three consecutive sentences beginning with “you.”
Hybrid is not always “the dog does X, the human does Y, together they do Z.”

Audience determines the relationship to the reader. The claim determines the
shape of the writing.

## Vary prose architecture deliberately

Before beginning a new card, notice the previous card's architecture:

- how it opened;
- whether it used a scene, declaration, question, or contrast;
- how many conceptual turns it made;
- where advice entered;
- how it ended;
- what kind of headline it used;
- how humor worked.

Then select a different architecture. Change pacing, sentence length,
paragraph movement, rhetorical stance, imagery, and ending strategy where the
claim allows it.

Before writing the opening, imagine the most obvious way to begin. If it
resembles generic horoscope prose or a completed card, choose the second or
third doorway instead. The direct opening may still be best when it is truly
specific; the purpose of this exercise is to interrupt autopilot.

Let paragraphs travel different roads. One may begin with observation, another
with surprise, misconception, question, contrast, or a practical situation.
Advice need not always arrive last. Explanation need not always sit between
observation and advice.

## Make headlines inseparable from their cards

A headline should arise from the remembered idea, not from the schema slot.
It may be playful, practical, intimate, surprising, or quietly descriptive,
but it should identify the particular insight.

Use the card-swap test: if two headlines could exchange cards without feeling
clearly wrong, at least one needs rewriting.

## Give humor a claim-specific premise

Before writing humor, answer:

> What is funny about this trait—not merely about dogs in general?

The comic premise may come from a recognizable contradiction, an exaggerated
but faithful priority, the dog's imagined interpretation of a household
ritual, or the disproportion between a small event and the importance the dog
assigns to it. Record that premise in the editorial plan.

Treats, squirrels, paperwork, management titles, and “I am a dog” jokes are not
inherently forbidden. They are simply not a substitute for finding the comedy
inside this claim.

## Seek grounded surprise

The strongest cards contain at least one thought that makes the reader pause:
an unexpected causal connection, a generous reinterpretation of an awkward
behavior, or a precise image that suddenly organizes several observations.

Surprise must remain supported by the supplied claim and evidence. It is not a
license to invent biography, diagnosis, or dramatic incidents. The goal is a
fresh angle on grounded material.

If the entire card could have been produced from a generic dog-personality
article, look again for the specific tension, cooperation, mode, or behavioral
sequence preserved by this claim.

## Surprise yourself

If, halfway through a story, you already know exactly how all remaining
sentences will unfold, you may be writing from a familiar structure rather
than from the claim.

Pause. Return to the remembered idea and editorial plan. Ask what would
genuinely surprise, delight, or clarify for the reader, then continue from
that discovery.

## Audit repeated lessons, not only repeated words

Lexical variation can hide conceptual duplication:

- “He prefers predictable routines.”
- “Stable expectations help him relax.”
- “Familiar structure gives him security.”

These may be the same lesson. Neighboring claims sometimes share motifs, but
each selected card still needs a distinct editorial job. When themes overlap,
differentiate the mechanism, situation, misunderstanding, developmental use,
or relationship consequence.

Retain semantic memory of completed cards so the deck does not keep teaching
the same lesson. Set aside their prose so they do not become templates.

Watch for theme leakage. A motif activated by one card—trust, adventure,
sensitivity, fairness, independence—can remain mentally prominent and spill
into every card that follows. If a neighboring story already teaches that
lesson well, let the current story emphasize a different truth unless its own
evidence genuinely requires the same theme. When it does recur, show a
different mechanism or consequence.

## Keep characterization consistent and expression diverse

Do not optimize for uniform writing style across cards. Optimize for a stable
understanding of the dog.

The same dog should appear throughout: the same underlying sensitivities,
drives, social tendencies, humor, and contradictions. But one card may read
like observational natural history, another like practical handling advice,
another like a warm conversation, another like a miniature character sketch,
and another like a shared domestic comedy.

Uniform prose is a defect. Diverse prose attached to contradictory
characterization is also a defect. The quality target is consistent
characterization expressed through varied forms.

## The checker establishes a floor

The deterministic checker detects selected failure signatures without
publishing its measurements or thresholds. An `accept` verdict does not
establish that the writing is vivid, insightful, natural, semantically
distinct, or enjoyable. It proves only that the pass cleared that mechanical
gate.

Write to the creative standard in this guide. Use the checker as a final
safety net, not as the target.

## Final independent-card test

Before considering a card complete, ask:

1. Could its prose architecture be exchanged with another card without much
   changing?
2. Does it contain a sentence I have used as a general-purpose explanation
   elsewhere?
3. Is its headline about this claim, or merely about its audience and
   astrology density?
4. Does each voice perform its audience purpose without relying on a stock
   grammar?
5. Does the card teach a distinguishable lesson?
6. Is there at least one concrete, memorable, or genuinely clarifying idea?
7. Does it sound like this dog?
8. What will the reader remember an hour later?
9. Does the humor emerge from this trait?

If the first three answers expose reuse, rewrite before moving on. The
deterministic checker catches copied language; this test catches the creative
repetition that copied-language detection cannot see.
